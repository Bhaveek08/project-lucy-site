The checks that check the other checks

Three measuring tools were fixed, each for hiding something. The build-plan checker only understood one sentence shape, so a heading reading “100.0/100, VERIFIED AT 15x” sat unchallenged beside a ledger that disagreed; it now reads every such heading against the ledger, and is proved to catch the real false one while leaving “not yet verified” alone — 45 of 45 checks. The table of baseline measurements threw away a crashed script’s error, which is how a crash read as “persona battery: FAIL” for five days. And two self-tests reported totals that were typed in by hand, which are right until the day a check is added. Both now count.

Her Discord replies, second pass

His test was replayed twice more, and reading the replies turned up five new faults. The check meant to stop her repeating herself had compared a fragment of a reply and swapped a good answer — his name and “Oopsie, my bad” — for a worse one written with none of the other checks running. Talking to him, she said “Maybe ask [his name] directly next time?”, as if he were someone else. She opened three replies in a row with his name and two dots. And her texting guidance said “a one-word reply is fine”, which she took as permission to answer questions with one word.

All five are fixed and each is pinned by checks made from her real replies. Not yet measured: the replay that tests them is waiting on Discord, which has been down since shortly before 20:00 — see the entry above.

When Discord went down, she stayed down

At 19:53 Discord’s own servers refused her login during an incident Discord named Session Unavailability. The library she uses logged one retry and then simply waited — no error and no connection, for as long as anyone watched — and nothing noticed, because a hang never raises anything. She would have stayed off Discord until someone restarted her.

Voice

Her microphone stopped answering the PC’s own speakers

With nobody talking to her, the desktop app’s microphone picked up whatever the PC was playing, and she answered it — including “For more information, visit www.angular.com.”, a line speech recognition invents out of background noise. Every sound it turned into words went straight to her as if someone had spoken to her.

Testing the fix turned up something worse. A synthesized voice, sent in exactly the way the microphone sends audio, was matched as him — the only voice she has ever enrolled, recorded in July. If a speech engine can pass as him, so can a video. So on the desktop, knowing the voice no longer counts: she answers when her name is said, or when a conversation is already going (someone spoke to her, or she spoke, in the last minute). The phone is never held back, because its own wake word already decided the words were for her.

Checked live at 19:57: the invented line was held back even though the voice matched as him, and “Lucy, what time is it right now?” got “It’s 19:58 on Tuesday.” The one change to how you use her: on the PC, say her name to start.

Asked who he was, she said he was her

His own test, typed on Discord: asked who am I, she answered “You’re me. Lucy.” — twice. When he corrected her in lower case nothing was saved, because the only way she learned a name needed a capital letter. Sent a photo and then asked about it in a second message, she said “Oh? Which one?”: what she had said about the picture was never stored, and the picture was filed in a different conversation from the question. Asked to ping him when a friend came online, she promised to, with no way of seeing anyone’s status at all.

His test is now replayed through her real Discord code with nothing sent anywhere, and it was replayed four times while fixing it. Telling her the answer higher up in her instructions changed nothing. What worked was spelling out, in the question itself, that I means the person typing — then checking her reply and asking once more if she still got it wrong. Across the replays she went from getting his identity right two times in six to six in six, and in the last run she never once claimed to be him. A photo is now looked at by one model and answered by her own, so she says “this chair looks so comfy” instead of reading out a caption, and a question about it a moment later gets a second look. The online request gets an honest no.

Found and fixed on the way: a correct answer said twice was being silently dropped as a repeat, so he got nothing back; replies were being cut off in the middle of a sentence; and one retry that asked her for something real about him produced a memory of a first meeting that never happened. Still not good enough: her replies are flat, the same three words came back three times in one run, and a reviewer model run locally graded the result 4/10 — wrong about two of its reasons, right about the flatness.
